Sensor rig planner · autonomous-driving data collection

Design data-collection rigs that record what your models need.

Vantage designs the sensor rigs of the vehicles that record training data for self-driving models. Place the cameras, lidars, radars, GNSS antennas and IMU on the vehicle and prove in simulation what the rig will record, before any hardware is fitted: what every lens sees, where the body, the rig and the other sensors' housings block it, how many pixels land on the road, what each lidar and radar reaches through the bumper, in rain, fog and at night, whether the recorder, the power supply and the cables keep up, and what a drive with it looks like to your pipeline. Then calibrate the real rig, check its recordings against the plan and hand the calibration to your data pipeline.

From CAD to calibrated rig

One tool from the first sketch to the first drive.

A data-collection rig is only as good as its weakest camera. Vantage covers the work from placing the cameras to checking them on the vehicle, so the numbers you plan with are the numbers you verify against.

Import the vehicle

glTF, OBJ, STL, PLY, FBX or STEP. A wizard sorts out units, axes, wheels, glass and the zones where cameras may go.

Place the sensors

Snap camera modules from the library onto the body, mirror them in pairs and aim them; add lidars, radars, GNSS antennas and an IMU; watch what the rig sees update as you go.

Simulate the recording

Simulated camera images, coverage on flat ground or a real road, pixel density, overlaps, the 3D blind volume, lidar and radar reach, weather and night, trigger timing, the data and power budgets, the cable harness, the GNSS sky, sun glare and a whole synthetic drive, checked against your requirements before anything is bought or drilled.

Fit, calibrate, verify

Print the brackets, check that the planned boards pin every camera, calibrate each camera from its live stream and each lidar to a camera, check the installed rig and its first recordings against the plan, follow every vehicle of the fleet, and keep the calibration files in step with the design.

Features

Know what the rig will record, before anything is drilled.

All videos on this page were recorded from the application itself, running the bundled Tata Tigor EV sample with six fisheye cameras.

Simulation

Coverage, resolution and redundancy, live

The ground around the vehicle is evaluated cell by cell for every camera: inside the lens's field of view, inside the image frame, not hidden by the body, the rig or another sensor's housing. For a data-collection rig that answers where the recording has holes, where it is too coarse to label, and where two cameras overlap. The result updates while you work.

  • Blind ground, coverage and redundancy on a 5, 2 or 1 cm grid inside an ROI drawn around the body or as a polygon.
  • Pixel density of the best camera at every point, in px/cm, against your minimum and target.
  • Per camera: what it sees, what only it sees, and what goes blind if it fails or is mounted badly.
  • An Issues list that checks cable lengths, GMSL ports and link budget, mounting zones and edge clearance.
Placement

Place cameras on the body, not in a spreadsheet

Cameras snap to the vehicle's surface with their housing standoff, and left/right pairs stay mirrored. Every field takes expressions and units, so 1880/2 mm means what it says.

  • Place on the surface (C), mirrored pairs with Alt-click, move (G) and rotate (R) gizmos, aim at a ground point.
  • The e-con Systems camera library or your own modules and lenses: rectilinear, fisheye models and measured Kannala–Brandt, with a datasheet field-of-view check.
  • An editable camera table with paste from Excel, coordinate frames from the rear axle, front axle or bumper, in m or mm.
  • Undo for everything, including scrubbing a value with the mouse as one step.
Camera library

Seventeen e-con Systems camera modules, every figure with its source

The library holds e-con Systems' automotive cameras: the enclosed STURDeCAM range, the NileCAM GMSL2 board cameras and the FPD-Link NeduCAM modules, 17 modules with 20 lenses. Each carries the figures e-con's product page states, the page and the date it was read, and a list of what the page does not give.

  • STURDeCAM20, 21, 25, 29, 31, 31FSH (heated fisheye), 34, 39, 57, 81 and 88; NileCAM81, 85, 130 and 210; NeduCAM25 (FPD-Link III) and Nedu4CAM57 (FPD-Link IV). Rolling and global shutter, colour, mono and RGB-IR, from 1920 × 1080 to 4208 × 3120.
  • e-con gives each lens's horizontal, vertical and diagonal field of view, not its focal length: the focal length is derived from it and the Kannala–Brandt model fitted to reproduce e-con's figures within 0.5° (the new lenses within 0.03°). Between those angles the lens is modelled, not measured; a unit calibration replaces it.
  • A figure the page does not state is left blank, never guessed; where the app needs one, such as a camera's power, it uses its typical figure.
  • The module editor shows the datasheet fields, the sources and the stated against the modelled field of view; its Preset list moves the cameras to any module and lens in one undo step.
3D view

Camera light: each lens's view, painted on the car

Turn on Camera light (Alt+3) and every camera lights up what it can see on the body and the ground, through its own lens model and around the body that occludes it. Overlaps show where two cameras meet.

  • A GPU shader renderer with shadows, contact shadow and smooth shading; it falls back to plain OpenGL and then to a software renderer.
  • Frustums, footprints, occlusion rays, seams and dimensions as overlays.
  • Render image at up to four times the view size and turntable videos for reviews.
3D analysis

The blind volume: where something can stand unseen

Ground coverage is not the whole story. Vantage samples the space around the car level by level and shows, in 3D, every pocket no camera sees, and how close to the body a pole, a child or an adult can stand without being detected.

  • Blind volume, the height up to which the ground is covered, and the undetected area for each object height.
  • A polar chart of how far from the body something can hide in every direction.
  • A height slider over a plan slice, and the blind space cut away in 3D at that height.
Perception range

How far out the rig resolves cars, people and signs

A detector needs an object to be a number of pixels tall. Vantage stands each object class at every distance in every direction and checks it through each camera's real lens and around the body: inside the image, not hidden, and tall enough. The result is the range at which your dataset will contain usable examples.

  • Car, pedestrian, cyclist and traffic sign with real sizes and your detector's minimum pixel height; add your own classes.
  • The range per direction against the range you need per sector, with the worst direction in each sector flagged. On the sample's six fisheyes a car is resolved to 70 m ahead, short of the default 80 m.
  • Camera pairs that overlap, with their baseline and the depth resolution a stereo pair would give at 10, 20 and 50 m.
  • Exported as JSON and a PNG plan; in batch mode with --perception-range.
Weather and night

How far the rig still sees in rain, fog and at night

Pick a condition on the perception range page and every range follows it: a camera loses an object once its contrast fades below 5 % or the headlamps and street lights leave it too dark for the sensor, and the lidars and radars lose range to the rain, fog and snow between them and it. The sample dataset renders the same condition.

  • On the sample's data rig an unlit night leaves pedestrians resolved to 19 m ahead (66 m by day) and nothing behind. The camera's darkest usable scene comes from its pixel size, f-number and frame rate; the headlamps are a typical low beam shaped after UN R112.
  • Heavy rain takes the long-range radar from 250 to 195 m (ITU-R P.838-3 at 77 GHz) and the roof lidar from 90 to 76 m; 150 m fog takes the lidar to 40 m, with false returns from droplets on about 11 % of its beams (a heuristic).
  • A panel compares every condition with clear: the range ahead per object, the sector checks that still pass, and each camera, lidar and radar. The clear envelope stays dashed on the map.
  • Exported as JSON, CSV and PNG; in batch mode with --weather, and --condition sets the weather for --perception-range and --dataset.

Typical models, not measurements. Glare from oncoming lights, the headlamps' own veil in fog, wet roads, spray and a wet radar fascia are not modelled.

Critical targets

What the rig must never miss, and how far each mount may be off

Place the objects that must be resolved where they matter: a 0.9 m child in front of the bumper, a pedestrian, a traffic light, a kerb by the front wheel, a lane marking on a curve. Vantage checks each through every camera's real lens, ray by ray around the body, the rig and the housings, then turns the question round: how far may each camera's mount be off before it loses one?

  • On the sample's data rig a child 2 m ahead spans 398 px of F's image (24 needed) and a child 1 m behind 614 px of R's. Only F resolves a pedestrian 10 m ahead: 153 px, with 146 lidar points per frame and three radars on it.
  • An overhead traffic light 15 m ahead is in no camera's image, though the lidar puts 30 points on it: the Issues list names it as serious.
  • Each axis is moved both ways until a target is lost, to 0.01° and 0.1 mm, then all six at once on a 729-pose grid. F may pitch 8.3° further down before it loses the pedestrian; moved back, it meets the bumper after 17 mm. Every camera holds at the ±10 mm, ±1° install tolerance.
  • The placement optimiser can require every target or weigh the missed ones. Exported as JSON, CSV and PNG with a mounting sheet for the bracket designers; in batch mode with --critical-targets, which exits with 2 when no camera resolves a target.

Sizes and pixel needs are typical figures, not a standard: set your detector's. Targets are flat and stand on level ground.

Rig parts

Roof racks and masts that block and carry cameras

A data-collection vehicle carries more than its body: crossbars, a roof rack, a sensor mast, an enclosure. Add them from the Vehicle menu, sized to the roof, and they hide what they hide in every analysis: the ground coverage, the blind volume, the camera images and the synthetic captures.

  • Boxes, cylinders and bars with position, size and yaw. Each part blocks the view, carries cameras, or both.
  • Cameras snap onto the parts that may carry them; a part marked as not mountable is a forbidden zone in the Issues list.
  • The rig goes out with the design: links in the ROS 2 URDF, a list in the extrinsics and a table in the report.
Lidar, radar and the recorder

The whole rig, not just its cameras.

A collection vehicle carries lidars and radars next to its cameras, and a recorder that has to keep up with all of them. Vantage places them on the same model, shows what they reach, what the vehicle blocks and what their housings hide from the cameras, times the cameras to the lidar's sweep and adds up the data before anything is bought.

Lidars and radars

Lidars and radars on the rig

Add a lidar or a radar from the Vehicle menu. A lidar goes on the mast head or the roof, a radar in the front bumper; move them in the inspector or drag them in the top view. The 3D view draws where each lidar channel meets the ground and each radar's field.

  • Ouster OS1-128, Hesai OT128, AT128 and XT32, Velodyne VLP-16, Livox Mid-360, and typical long-range, imaging and corner radars. Figures are nominal datasheet values, each with its source; edit them to match your unit.
  • Import the measured beam angles from an Ouster metadata file, a Hesai angle-correction CSV or a Velodyne calibration.
  • The Issues list flags a lidar inside the body and a radar set deeper than a bumper fascia.
  • Every sensor frame goes into the URDF and TF launch file, the extrinsics, the nuScenes tables and KITTI, including the lidar-to-camera file KITTI tools expect.
Housings

Sensor housings block the cameras too

Every lidar, radar, GNSS antenna and IMU housing, and every other camera's module, is a solid at its real size and pose that blocks each camera exactly like the body and the rig: in the ground coverage, the blind volume, the perception range, the simulated images, the camera light and the installation check.

  • On the sample's data rig the front radar RD1 sits just in front of the bumper, below camera F: its housing covers 13.7 % of F's image, and the blind ground in the ROI goes from 0.010 m² with the body and rig alone to 0.240 m². Moved 5 cm back behind the fascia, it is out of the image.
  • The Inspector shows the share of the selected camera's image each housing covers, and where. The Issues list warns above 2 % or at the image centre, is serious above 10 % and critical for a lens inside another housing.
  • Analysis ▸ Housings block cameras switches them off to compare with the body and rig alone. A housing set behind the bumper fascia or into a panel stays hidden by the body.
  • Exported as JSON and CSV with the coverage both ways; in batch mode with --housings.

Housings are the datasheets' outer boxes and cylinders; cables, brackets and connectors are not modelled.

Lidar and radar coverage

How many kinds of sensor see each object

For every direction and object class, how far the cameras, the lidars and the radars detect it, against the range your dataset needs. A sector two or more of them cover is redundant, one is single, none is a gap.

  • Lidar points counted on each object from the real beam elevations, around the body and the rig; radar ranges scaled by each object's cross-section and by what the radar looks through: on the sample the corner radars lose 2.67 dB through the painted bumper fascia (below).
  • What the vehicle blocks: on the sample, 35 % of a lidar sitting on the roof hits the roof, and its nearest ground point is 8.5 m or more from the body. Raised 0.6 m, it loses none.
  • How much of each camera image the lidar covers, for projecting points into images.
  • Exported as JSON and a PNG plan; in batch mode with --sensor-coverage.
Sync and timing

Cameras that fire when the lidar looks

Each camera should expose its image while the spinning lidar sweeps across its view. Vantage works out when the sweep crosses every camera, the trigger that centres a rolling-shutter exposure on it, and what sharing a trigger line costs.

  • A dial of one lidar rotation with every camera's field, sweep crossing and trigger.
  • Suggested trigger lines: on the sample's data rig, six cameras on one line are up to 12.4 ms off the sweep (17 cm at 50 km/h); four lines bring that under 1 ms.
  • Phases for further lidars, motion blur and rolling-shutter skew at speed, and the longest exposure that keeps blur under a pixel.
  • Exported as JSON and the dial; in batch mode with --sync-plan.

These are planned times. The Live rig page measures the real skew, from software timestamps.

Recording budget

Will the recorder keep up?

Every camera's, lidar's and radar's data rate, the load on each link and on the uplink to the recorder, the write speed the disks need, and how many hours of driving fit on a disk set.

  • Cameras raw, JPEG or H.265; lidars as driver packets or decoded clouds; GNSS, IMU and CAN on top.
  • On the sample, six raw cameras record 8.5 Gbit/s: an 8 TB disk set lasts 2 hours, which the Issues list reports.
  • A recorder short of write speed, or a lidar on a link it saturates, is a critical issue.
  • Exported as JSON and CSV; in batch mode with --recording-budget, which exits with 2 when something cannot keep up.

The JPEG and H.265 bits per pixel are assumptions: replace them with figures measured on your encoder.

Before the rig is built

Power, cables, sky, sun, materials and brackets, checked on the model.

The rest of what decides whether a rig works on the road: whether the vehicle can power it and every cable reaches, whether its GNSS antennas see the sky and its INS knows its lever arms, which cameras will stare into the sun and which lenses will be dirty, what the radars look through, and the brackets that put each camera where the plan says.

Power budget

What the rig draws, and whether the supply carries it

Every camera, lidar, radar, GNSS antenna and INS on the rig, with the computer, recorder and switch, added up steady and at switch-on. Vantage checks the DC-DC converter, each fused circuit and each power cable's voltage drop, the alternator's margin while driving and how long an auxiliary battery runs the rig with the engine off.

  • On the sample's data rig with an INS and two antennas: 183 W steady and 236 W at switch-on, 199 W from the vehicle. That is 31 % of a 600 W converter, 491 W spare on the alternator and 4 hours on a 1 kWh battery.
  • Fuses sized for you (steady current within 80 %, the switch-on peak within the rating) or typed in: the compute circuit draws 5.7 A, so a 5 A fuse blows; on a 24 V bus the same circuit draws 2.8 A.
  • Power cables routed from the ECU, with the voltage drop at constant power against a 5 % limit and the device's minimum input, and the wire gauge that would fix it.
  • Library power figures are nominal and the peak factors typical: replace them with your units' datasheets. Exported as JSON and CSV; in batch mode with --power-budget.
Cable harness

Every cable routed along the body, against its link's reach

Each camera's and sensor's cable runs along the body's outer surface to an entry point (a roof grommet, a door seal, the bulkhead grommet at the windscreen's base, the tailgate) and on inside along the trim to the compute box. The surface path is the cheapest on the body, kept off glass, doors and shut lines and pulled straight: exact on a plane, within 1.5 % of the shortest path across an edge.

  • On the sample's data rig the 18 cables come to 56.2 m. The front camera needs 3.64 m over the bonnet to the bulkhead grommet, where the estimate along the axes said 2.95 m: its 3 m cable does not reach.
  • Each run against its link: GMSL2 and FPD-Link coax (15 m and the module's cable options), 100/1000BASE-T1, CAN FD, RS-422; GNSS coax as an RF loss budget at L1 against the antenna amplifier's gain; power pairs by the power budget's voltage drop. The limits are typical, each with its basis.
  • One switch makes the routed lengths replace the estimate in the Issues list, the camera cable check and the power budget; View ▸ Overlays ▸ Cable harness draws the routes in the main views.
  • The sync plan also checks the deserializers: a stereo pair or a trigger line split across two is a warning, with the port move that fixes it. Exported as JSON with every path in the vehicle frame, CSV and PNG; in batch mode with --harness, which exits with 2 when a cable cannot reach.

The vehicle model has no interior: the inside run is the trim path along the axes with the routing slack. Bend radii, connectors at the grommets and bundles are not modelled.

GNSS and IMU

Open sky for the antennas, lever arms for the INS

Every GNSS antenna's sky is traced past the body, the rig and the other sensors, so you see what each mast and lidar takes before the antenna is bolted on. The same page gives the dual-antenna heading baseline, the lever arms your INS needs and how well each sensor's world pose will be known.

  • A sky plot per antenna with every blocker named. With the sample's lidar raised on a mast, an antenna on the roof keeps 98 % of the sky above 10°; clamped beside the mast it loses 10 % and is flagged for multipath.
  • Expected satellites assume satellites spread evenly over the sky (32 above the mask is a typical multi-constellation figure), so read them as an indicator; multipath is a geometric rule, not a signal simulation.
  • Heading baseline, direction and heading error from the INS's rated figure: 1.15 m gives 0.17° with a typical 0.1°-at-2 m unit. Lever arms come in the IMU and vehicle frames, plus a plain text file for the INS tool.
  • A pose error budget per camera, lidar and radar (about 32 mm with a typical 2 cm RTK INS). Exported as JSON, text and PNG; in batch mode with --gnss.
Sun glare and dirt

Which cameras look into the sun, and when

Give the place, the dates, the driving hours and the route's headings. Vantage puts the sun at every sampled time and heading through each camera's real lens and the body, and shows how much of the driving time it is in the image, by month, hour and heading. A heuristic score says which lenses and sensor windows wheel spray, the rear wake and insects will dirty.

  • Sun position from the NOAA algorithm, checked against published examples to 0.003°. Clear sky and a flat horizon are assumed.
  • On the sample in Munich, the front and rear cameras have the sun in the image 7.2 % of a year's driving (264 h), mostly November to February in the morning and afternoon; pitched 10° further down, 3.0 %. On an east–west route, 9.7 %.
  • A month × hour heatmap, the sky seen from each camera (its field, the body, where the sun goes), the sun's path through the image on the equinox and the solstices, and the flare risk just outside the image.
  • A dirt and spray score for each lens, lidar window and radar fascia (a heuristic with stated weights), with cleaning advice and a map of the body. Exported as JSON and PNG; in batch mode with --exposure.
Materials, glass and wipers

What the radars look through, and the cameras behind glass

Every part of the vehicle has a material: painted steel, a painted bumper fascia, plastic trim, a radar cover, laminated glass, each with typical 77 GHz figures from published measurements and their source. Vantage traces each radar's field to what lies in front of it and works out the loss layer by layer (plastic, primer, basecoat, clear coat) at every ray's angle. The coverage and the critical targets use the shortened ranges.

  • On the sample's data rig the corner radars RD2–RD5 sit 12 mm behind the fascia: 2.67 dB lost there and back with a solid paint, so their range on a car drops from 100 to 86 m. Metallic paint costs 3.9 dB (80 m), a repainted metallic bumper 8.3 dB (62 m). RD1, in front of the bumper, loses nothing.
  • The fascia's thickness in half wavelengths and the nearby thickness that reflects least (3.6 mm here), the radar–fascia gap against its resonances, and metal in or beside the beam, summed up as a multipath risk.
  • A camera behind the windscreen: added to the sample, it keeps 73 % of the light at 67° to the glass, its centre appears 3.8 mm off (0.25 px left at 3 m), and the glass reflects a third of the dashboard's brightness into its image, a warning until a glare shield is ticked.
  • Wipers sized from the windscreen, or your own arms, with each windscreen camera's footprint on the glass, red where no blade passes: typical tandem wipers clear all of that camera's view. Exported as JSON, CSV and PNG; in batch mode with --materials, which exits with 2 when a radar looks into metal.

Material figures are typical literature values, not your vehicle's: measure your fascia and enter it. Surfaces are flat layers at each ray, and the multipath risk is an indicator.

Mount brackets

A printed bracket that holds each camera where you planned it

For every camera Vantage designs a 3D-printable bracket: a plate on the housing, a solid support and a base fitted to the panel under it, or a clamp on a rig tube. It sizes them so the optical centre and the angles land on the plan, which it then measures back from the finished mesh.

  • Most of the sample's cameras sit 15–22 mm off the body: a 44 mm housing would go into the panel. The page says a printed bracket needs 41–47 mm, and Move out puts the camera there; a mirrored partner follows.
  • Measured back from the mesh, the optical centre lands within 0.01 mm of the plan and the plate within 0.01°. Every STL is checked closed and consistently wound: about 160,000 triangles at the 0.35 mm grid.
  • Checks for the share of the image the bracket blocks (the camera's own pixel rays traced against it), the thinnest wall, the lever arm and gaps under the base on a curved panel. Stiffness is a beam estimate and the VHB pad follows 3M's rule of thumb.
  • Exported as an STL per camera, JSON with each bracket's frame and checks, and a dimensioned PNG sheet; in batch mode with --brackets.

The housing is modelled as a box of the module's size, with the optical centre at the lens front. Check your module's drawing and hole pattern before printing.

Requirements scorecard

Signed off against what the programme asked for

Write the programme's requirements as checks on Vantage's own studies, such as "pedestrians resolved to 40 m in every sector", "8 h of driving per disk set" or "camera–lidar misalignment at most 1 ms", and get one verdict for the rig. Every value comes from the study that computes it, with its figures one click away. A must requirement that fails fails the rig.

  • Add from the plan turns what is already set into requirements. On the sample's data rig with an INS that is 24 of them, from the perception range's required ranges to the recording shift, the power runtime and the GNSS sky. Templates add more, and the project brief's phrasings with a number and a unit are proposed for review, never added by themselves.
  • Run computes each study once, in the background: 25 requirements over 10 studies in under 2 s. On the sample, one trigger line leaves a camera 12.4 ms off the lidar sweep; the sync plan's suggested lines bring that to 0.94 ms, and the requirement passes.
  • A design-review pack as PDF: the verdict, the scorecard, a page per study with its figures and plot, the Issues list, and every study's assumptions. JSON and CSV come with it.
  • In CI, --check writes the same files and exits with 5 when a must requirement fails or cannot be evaluated.
Also for production surround-view systems

The same engine, for the in-car display.

If the rig, or the vehicle it prototypes, has a surround-view display, Vantage designs that too: the stitched bird's-eye view and its seams, the 3D bowl, parking guidelines and what stitching does to standing people.

Surround view

The in-car display, before the car exists

Vantage renders a synthetic parking scene through every camera's real lens, stitches the images into the bird's-eye view and projects them onto a 3D bowl around the car, exactly as a surround-view ECU would.

  • Seams you can drag, exposure balancing and multi-band blending; the bowl's floor, wall, reach and profile are tunable.
  • Remap tables per camera for the bird's-eye view, with a dependency-free C++17 and a Python reference stitcher that match Vantage to within a grey level.
  • Export the bowl as OBJ and texture with per-vertex remap tables for a 3D renderer on the ECU.
Parking guidelines

Trajectory lines and the swept path, through every lens

A kinematic model of the vehicle turns the steering angle into the lines a production display draws: distance markers, the bumper corners' trajectory in red, yellow and green, and the ground the whole body, mirrors included, passes over.

  • Drawn through each camera's real lens: they curve with the fisheye and disappear behind the body.
  • On the camera images, the bird's-eye view, the bowl and the 3D view; obstacles in the swept path are flagged with the distance to contact.
  • Exported per camera for the ECU: pixel polylines for every steering angle from lock to lock.
Stitched view

Where people vanish at the seams

A bird's-eye view assumes everything lies on the ground, so a standing person is smeared away from each camera. Near a seam both smears can land in the other camera's sector, and the person disappears from the display even though both raw images show them.

  • A pole, a 0.9 m child or a 1.8 m adult is stood on every point and followed through the same seams and blending as the remap tables.
  • The map separates vanishes at a seam from cut off by the view's edge, marks hotspots and shows the smears under the cursor.
  • Suggest seams searches each seam ±15° for angles that hide less, without losing ground density; Apply is one undo step.
Robustness

Designs that survive the real world.

A camera is never mounted exactly where it was drawn, a loaded car sits lower, and the road is not flat. These studies tell you how much that matters, and which camera to worry about.

Tolerance, optimiser, variants

  • Monte Carlo tolerance study with a sensitivity tornado and the worst cases pinned for inspection.
  • Placement optimiser: an evolutionary search scored under tolerance, with a Pareto front.
  • Variants side by side with a difference map and a table of every figure.
  • Load cases from the suspension and the real loads, and the road and body motion: below.

Load cases

How far the cameras move when the car is loaded

The cameras are calibrated at one load; the crew, the luggage and the rig itself change it. From the suspension (springs and motion ratios, tyres, bump stops, anti-roll bars, measured wheel-load curves) and the masses where they sit, a static solve gives each corner's drop, the body's pitch and roll, and every camera's and sensor's move against the calibration load.

  • Seats, boot, roof, the recording equipment and the rig's own cameras, sensors and parts as masses at points, estimated or entered. An off-centre load rolls the body.
  • On the sample's data rig with typical values: the driver alone rolls the body 0.27° and moves a quarter camera and a corner radar 16 mm; fully loaded, the rear sits on its bump stops and the rear camera moves 85 mm. Run adds the ground figures: the stitched ground shifts up to 65 cm, and the seams ghost by 35 cm.
  • Suspension data from JSON, YAML, or a CSV parameter sheet or ride-height table, units converted (N/mm, lbf/in; kg, lb; mm, in). Typed axle drops still work.
  • Exported as JSON and CSV; in batch mode with --suspension and --suspension-data.

Until you enter your vehicle's suspension, the values are typical of a compact sedan scaled to the model, and the dialog says so. Static loads only: no braking, cornering or kinematic wheel paths.

Road and body motion

The rig on a real road: crests, speed bumps, braking and cornering

Put the car on a road instead of flat ground: a ramp, a crest, a garage ramp, a speed bump, a kerb, a crowned road. The car stands on its four tyres, and every heat map, figure and Issue uses the road: a point counts only where the camera's ray clears the road itself. Body motion puts braking, accelerating and cornering through the suspension.

  • On the sample, a crest 5 m ahead (a 300 m curve into an 8 % fall) keeps the road ahead in view to 20 m, where flat ground is seen to the end of the 60 m check; the side-on profile shows what drops out of view.
  • An 80 mm speed bump is first resolved 20 m ahead on level road, when its height spans 3 px in F's image; beyond that crest, only at 18 m. The body's underside and every sensor are checked for clearance standing and while driving over each bump, kerb and ramp.
  • Hard braking at 0.8 g pitches the body 2.25° and moves F's horizon 36 px; cornering at 0.5 g rolls it 2.55°; braking in a bend shifts the stitched ground 75 cm. The suspension, the centre of gravity and the anti-dive figures are typical until you enter yours.
  • Exported as JSON, CSV and PNG; in batch mode with --terrain, which exits with 2 when the body or a sensor would touch the road.

Body motion is steady state: no dampers or overshoot. The optimiser, the tolerance study, the perception range, the installation check and the load cases keep to flat ground.

Fit and calibrate

Calibrate the real cameras from their live streams

Point Vantage at each camera's stream (an RTSP or HTTP URL, a V4L2 device, or a GStreamer pipeline such as a Jetson's GMSL cameras), lay the planned checkerboards on the floor, and check every installed camera against the plan from one frame each.

  • Boards are found in rectified patches cut out of the fisheye image with the exact lens model, and the pose is solved per camera.
  • A joint solve refines all cameras together with each board's placement, so neighbouring cameras agree at the seams. It now reaches its optimum every time, in about 0.15 s for six cameras instead of 3.4 s, and its answers scatter as its own uncertainty predicts (checked over 40 simulated installations).
  • Per camera: Δx Δy Δz, Δyaw Δpitch Δroll and a verdict against your tolerance; the measured poses become an as-built variant.
  • Unit lens calibration from 10–20 board views (cv::fisheye), or import calibrations from OpenCV, ROS camera_info, Kalibr or JSON.

Live rig health

Every stream, its health, and whether a camera moved

Calibrate ▸ Live rig reads every camera's stream at once and says, per camera, whether it is delivering what the recording needs: frame rate against the module's, interval jitter, dropped frames, exposure and clipping, sharpness and frozen images, and across the rig how far apart the cameras' frames arrive.

  • Drift indicator: the planned body outline and horizon drawn over the live image through the lens model. If the body edge sits elsewhere, the camera has probably been knocked; the installation check then measures by how much.
  • Save frames or hand them straight to the installation check.
  • Before each run: vantage rig.vantage --rig-health 10 health.json exits with 3 if a camera is faulty.
  • Stream passwords are kept in a private file in your settings folder, never in the project.

Capture times are when frames reached Vantage, not sensor timestamps, so network and encoder delays are included. The recording uses synthetic streams rendered from the plan.

Synthetic streams
Lidar–camera calibration

Where the lidar really sits, from a board both sensors see

Hold a printed checkerboard up at the poses Vantage plans. The camera finds its corners through its real lens model; the lidar sees the board's plane, its edge and its squares. One solve gives the lidar's pose relative to the camera, and so in the vehicle, against the plan and with its uncertainty.

  • A pose plan that places and turns each board so that together they pin all six degrees of freedom. On the sample, the board planes alone would put the roof lidar within ±4.2 / ±6.3 / ±16.4 mm: the camera's corners, 4–8 m away through a fisheye, fix each board's distance only to centimetres. That prediction now matches 300 simulated calibrations within 7 %; the edges and the checker do far better.
  • Try it on synthetic captures through the real lens and the lidar's beams: a lidar mounted 15 mm forward, 8 mm up and 0.6° turned measures 15.2 mm, 10.2 mm and 0.61° off the plan, 2.2 mm and 0.021° from the true mounting, with a 1σ of ±2.1 mm. The solve takes 0.1 s, and a bias of about 3 mm that range noise along oblique beams used to cause is gone.
  • Real image and point-cloud pairs from a folder: PCD, KITTI and nuScenes .bin, PLY, NumPy, CSV.
  • The lidar's points on the image through the plan and through the solve. Boards that all lean the same way are reported as leaving a direction undetermined, not given a number. Apply the measured pose or keep it as a new variant; in batch mode with --lidar-calib.
Calibration observability

How firmly the planned boards will pin each sensor, before any is printed

Vantage asks how precisely the corners of the planned boards, each found to a fraction of a pixel through the camera's lens model, will fix where every camera sits, with the boards laid by hand a little off their plan as the installation check's joint solve assumes. Weak directions are explained in plain words, and fixes are tried and measured before they are offered.

  • On the sample's eight floor boards every camera's yaw comes out at ±0.13° (1σ), 1.3× a 0.1° requirement. The corners alone would give ±0.01°, so where the boards lie is what limits it: laid against a jig (±5 mm, ±0.25°) the worst axis drops to 0.5×.
  • Cameras that share a board are tied far tighter than either is to the vehicle (F to ML at 0.5× the requirement). Lidar–camera pairs use the lidar–camera calibration's boards and say whether the corners or the lidar's points limit them; radars use the corner reflectors you place.
  • Verify solves simulated captures with the solves that will solve the real ones and compares their spread with the prediction. The fusion tab maps where lidar points land in each image: on 22 % of F's view of a surface 20 m out, about one point per 37 pixels there.
  • Apply moves or adds a suggested board in one undo step. Exported as JSON, CSV and PNG; in batch mode with --observability (and --verify N), which exits with 2 when a camera sees no board whole.

Predictions from the geometry, not measurements: the lens models are taken as exact and the corner noise as equal on every corner.

Drive-log check

The recorded drive, checked against the plan

Open a recorded drive, a ROS 2 bag or a nuScenes folder, and Vantage checks it against the rig you planned: every stream there at its rate, the cameras triggered in step with the lidar sweep, and the lidar points landing on the right pixels. The log is read once and never loaded whole, so long drives are fine.

  • Streams: rate, drops, gaps, jitter, latency and image size per sensor, with a timeline of the gaps. On a synthetic drive of the sample's data rig with faults put in, it finds one camera's 4 dropped frames (9.1 %) and another camera's missing topic.
  • Sync: the lidar's measured phase lock from its points' own times, and each camera's own trigger error, apart from the lidar's. In the same drive, the camera triggered 7 ms late reads 7.0 ms, the others 0.0 ms.
  • Rotations: lidar depth edges are matched to image edges to find each camera's rotation relative to the lidar, with a confidence. The front camera, mounted 0.6° off in yaw, comes out at +0.53°, the others within 0.1°. This is an estimate from edge alignment; translation is not measured this way.
  • Apply the confident rotations as a corrected variant in one step. Exported as JSON, CSV and PNG overlays; in batch mode with --drive-log.
As-built check

Does the real rig see what the plan says?

Open a short drive recorded by the real rig: a ROS 2 bag, a nuScenes folder or a folder of images per camera. What stays the same while the vehicle moves is the vehicle itself. Vantage lays each camera's real outline of it over the simulated one, fits the mounting error that lines them up, and checks whether the plan's coverage still holds with the rig as built.

  • The ego mask comes from frames taken while moving (odometry, GNSS or the picture changing). Plain road and sky are told from the body by static edges and the planned silhouette; what cannot be decided is left out, not guessed.
  • On a synthetic drive of the sample's data rig with the left mirror camera mounted 2° low and rolled 1°, ML comes out at pitch +1.99° ± 0.02° and roll +1.09° ± 0.10°. The rear camera, turned 1.5°, cannot be told from its outline (its bumper is the road's grey), so the drive-log estimate against the lidar stands in: +1.44°.
  • Axes the outline cannot pin stay as planned; a camera that sees too little of the vehicle is marked not checkable, with the reason.
  • The corrected copy's coverage against the plan, and a verdict: holds, holds with corrections, does not hold. Keep it as an as-built variant in one step; in batch mode with --as-built-check.

Validated on synthetic recordings, where the injected errors came back within about 1.5 standard errors. Real lenses, glossy paint and body meshes add errors of their own.

Fleet

The same rig on every vehicle, month after month

A collection programme builds one rig on several vehicles and calibrates them again and again. Import each vehicle's installation checks and calibrations, and Vantage compares every sensor with the plan, follows it over time, and names the vehicle that differs from the rest, before a loose bracket spoils a dataset.

  • Reads installation check reports, Vantage's extrinsics in any export frame and unit, nuScenes calibration tables and Kalibr camchains (camera-to-camera only, so a Kalibr chain is placed on the plan by a rigid fit). Tags are matched to the plan; unknown ones are reported.
  • A matrix of vehicles × sensors: each sensor's latest offset against the tolerance (10 mm / 1° by default), as OK, adjust or re-mount.
  • Drift: a straight line through the dated records, counted only when it is statistically significant, with the days until it leaves the tolerance. In the clip's simulated fleet, a front camera pitching down 0.2° a month is found and crosses the band. A trend is a warning, not a forecast.
  • A vehicle that stands out from the fleet median is named (a robust z-score: here a mirror camera mounted 2° yawed on one of five). When the whole fleet is built off the plan, one click makes an as-built variant from the fleet median. Exported as JSON, CSV and PNG; in batch mode with --fleet.
AI assistant

Ask the design questions. Get answers from the analysis.

The assistant runs on a model hosted on Groq with your own API key. It reads the design only through Vantage's own tools, so every number in an answer comes from the analysis, and it can only propose changes.

From a question to a measured proposal

  • Answers with reasons: why ground is blind (field of view, image frame, the body, a rig part or a sensor housing in the way), which camera matters most, what the studies show.
  • Agent mode: give it a goal; it plans a checklist, runs studies, searches camera poses, and proposes the best change with its effect.
  • Proposals show the change and the predicted effect; apply them as one undo step or as a new variant, and the card shows the measured result.
  • A project brief with your requirements goes with every question; slash commands such as /optimise QR and /compare.

Only camera poses, lens and module names and analysis figures are sent to Groq, never the vehicle model, images or files; every lookup is listed with what it sent. In this recording the model's replies were scripted; the lookups, the optimisation and the proposal were computed live by Vantage.

Datasets

Calibration in your dataset's format, checked against its tools

The calibration your data pipeline reads, written straight from the design or from the measured as-built poses, in the vehicle frame and origin you choose. Each format has been checked against the tool that reads it, not only against Vantage's own maths.

  • nuScenes sensor.json and calibrated_sensor.json: cameras, lidars and radars to ego; the nuScenes devkit's own geometry reproduces Vantage's projection.
  • KITTI calib_cam_to_cam.txt with P_rect for the rectified pinhole image the README's cv2.fisheye remap makes: the KITTI devkit's projection lands lidar points within 0.001 px in every camera. With a lidar, an object-benchmark calib file per camera, as mmdetection3d and OpenPCDet read it.
  • ROS camera_info per camera, read back exactly by ROS 2's camera_calibration_parsers. A Kalibr camchain read by Kalibr's own config reader, each lens in the model that reproduces it (double sphere, omni or extended unified past 90°, with the error stated), and T_cam_imu with an IMU.
  • Where a tool's own assumption does not fit a surround rig (pykitti's stereo layout, image_geometry's Python model ignoring the fisheye model), the file and its README say so.
Sample dataset

A drive your pipeline can ingest before the rig exists

Vantage drives the planned rig down a synthetic street and records what each sensor would: the cameras through their real lenses at the sync plan's trigger times, the lidar column by column as it spins, the radars, GNSS and IMU. The drive is written as a nuScenes version folder and a ROS 2 bag, so ingestion, labelling tools and training loaders can be tested against the real sensor set, frames, timing and calibration.

  • On the sample's data rig, an 8 s drive at 30 km/h gives 478 images, 80 lidar sweeps and 765 radar scans, with 615 annotated boxes on 16 keyframes: about 1.5 minutes and 270 MB.
  • The nuScenes devkit loads it as it is. On rectified images its lidar-to-image projection lands within 0.002 px of Vantage's own. The calibration is the nuScenes export's.
  • Lidar sweeps are not motion compensated, as a driver delivers them. Boxes carry visibility and lidar and radar point counts.
  • The bag has images, camera_info, points, detections, IMU, GNSS fixes, /tf and ground-truth markers, with the URDF's frame ids; in batch mode with --dataset.

The scene is synthetic: simple shapes and typical material reflectivities, one sun, and fog, rain, snow or night from the typical weather models when you pick them. Use it to test a pipeline, not to train a model.

Automation and CI

The calibration files stay in step with the design, and your checks run on them

Switch on Export ▸ Automation, and on every save, after a quiet period or by hand Vantage regenerates the chosen exports into a folder next to the project, runs the requirements check, writes a summary of what changed and then runs your own command, such as make test.

  • The command is kept only in your settings on this computer, never in the project file. It is shown in full and runs only once you approve it; changing it asks again. The assistant can see whether one is set, never its text, and cannot start a run.
  • Changes are found by content, not time stamps: on the sample 26 files are written in under a second, and with camera F moved 5 cm, 10 of them change (F's OpenCV file, the extrinsics, URDF, TF, nuScenes, KITTI and Kalibr files, the requirements results) while the other cameras' files stay as they were. Files a removed camera no longer produces are removed; yours never are.
  • The command gets VANTAGE_EXPORT_DIR, VANTAGE_SUMMARY (every file with its SHA-256, what changed, the requirements verdict) and VANTAGE_CHECK_EXIT; its output streams into the page with the exit code, the duration and a history of runs.
  • Headless, vantage --watch rig.vantage --on-change "make test" does the same after every change of the file; the CI templates run --export and --check in GitHub Actions or GitLab CI.

The command runs with your permissions: approving its exact text is the only gate. Keep passwords and tokens in environment variables, not in the command.

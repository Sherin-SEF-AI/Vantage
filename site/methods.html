Technical reference

The models behind Vantage

What each of Vantage's models computes and how: its equations, frames and units, the assumptions it makes, the parameters it takes with their typical values and where they come from, how it is checked against independent references, where it stops being right, and the papers and standards it follows. The documentation says how to use the app; this page says what its numbers mean.

How to read this

Every study in Vantage turns a design (the vehicle, the rig and its settings) into figures: a blind area in square metres, a perception range in metres, a 1σ in degrees. For each model this page gives:

  • What it computes and from which inputs, in which frame and units.
  • The equations, in the form the code evaluates them. Symbols are defined where they first appear.
  • Assumptions and parameters: what is modelled and what is not; each parameter's typical value and its source. A figure called typical is a representative value from the literature or a product class, not a measurement of your hardware; the app says so wherever it uses one.
  • Validation: the checks of the validation suite that compare the model with an independent reference, with the error measured and the tolerance. The table under each model is from the run named in its caption; run the suite yourself to reproduce it.
  • Limits: where the model is an approximation, how far off it can be, and what it leaves out.

Every export carries the version of Vantage and the inputs that produced it, so a figure can be traced back to the model described here. Where a model is a documented approximation (a fitted lens, a heuristic dirt score), the study's own page and JSON state it next to the figure.

The validation suite builds these rotations with scipy's Rotation.from_euler('ZYX', …) rather than the app's own, and checks the coverage footprints against them (coverage.oblique_footprint).

The validation suite

The suite runs every model against references that do not share its code, and writes a report: the quantity compared, the reference, the error measured, the tolerance and the verdict for each check, the numbers behind it, a figure where one helps, the references cited and the versions of everything that ran.

python -m vantage.validation report/                 # every check, the built-in sample
python -m vantage.validation report/ --project rig.vantage   # occlusion and exports on your vehicle and rig
python -m vantage.validation report/ --quick --models lens,weather
python -m vantage.validation --list                  # the checks, their references and tolerances

It writes validation.html (a standalone page with the figures), validation.json (every number), validation.csv (a row per check) and figures/*.svg, and exits 0 when every check passes and 6 when one fails, so it can gate a build. A full run takes about a minute; --quick uses fewer Monte Carlo trials (same tolerances) for a smoke run.

Kinds of reference. A closed form written out again in the check (not imported from the model); another library's implementation of the same model (OpenCV's pinhole and fisheye projections, scipy's root finders, rotations and binomial intervals); a published table or worked example (ITU-R P.838-3's Table 5, ITU-R P.1814's tables, UN R112's test points, Meeus's and the SPA paper's examples); a measurement (e-con's stated fields of view, a measured painted bumper plate); brute force (exact ray casting against every triangle, a dense march along every line of sight, every lidar beam cast); or Monte Carlo with the machinery that solves real captures.

Statistics. Monte Carlo checks compare an estimated spread with a prediction. A standard deviation estimated from n trials scatters by about 1/√(2(n − 1)) in its logarithm, so these checks pass when every axis's |log(empirical σ / predicted σ)| is within 4.5 such standard errors and every camera's mean squared Mahalanobis distance lies within four standard deviations of its degrees of freedom. Every random draw comes from the run's seed and the check's id, so a run reproduces bit for bit and one check's draws do not depend on which others ran.

What it does not show. That a model describes your vehicle, lens or sensor: that needs measurements of them (the as-built check and the drive-log check compare a recording with the plan). The suite shows that each model computes what this page says it computes, within the stated tolerance, for the cases it builds.

Lens models

What it computes. The image height r(θ) (mm on the sensor) of a ray θ radians off the optical axis, and from it a pixel for every direction and a direction for every pixel. Module: core/lens.py.

rectilinear r = f tan θ · equidistant r = f θ · equisolid r = 2f sin(θ/2) · stereographic r = 2f tan(θ/2) · orthographic r = f sin θ The classical projections (Miyamoto 1964).
Kannala–Brandt: r(θ) = f θ (1 + k1θ² + k2θ⁴ + k3θ⁶ + k4θ⁸) The model of OpenCV's fisheye module (θd = r/f); every built-in lens and every unit calibration uses it.
u = cx + x/ρ · r(θ)/p,   v = cy + a · y/ρ · r(θ)/p,   θ = atan2(ρ, z),  ρ = √(x² + y²) (x, y, z) a direction in the optical frame, p the pixel pitch (mm), a = fy/fx the pixel aspect (1 for a library lens). A direction is in the image when (u, v) lies on the sensor and θ ≤ θmax, the image circle. The inverse solves r(θ) = r in closed form, or by Newton's method for Kannala–Brandt.

The built-in library. e-con Systems' public pages state each lens's horizontal, vertical and diagonal field of view but not its focal length or distortion. A built-in lens is therefore made from the stated FOV: the base projection that alone best reproduces the stated (H, V, D) points gives the focal length (least squares of r = f·g(θ) relative to each half size of the sensor), and k1…k4 are fitted to the stated points (weighted up to 50 : 1 against each sample of the base curve, less where so strong a fit would fold back) together with the base curve sampled densely, so that r(θ) keeps rising out to the field's edge. The stated FOV is a measurement e-con publishes; the curve between the stated angles is a model. Where a stated diagonal cannot be the sensor diagonal's field (a 210° lens on a sensor 196° wide), it is taken as the image circle and limits θmax. Otherwise θmax = D/2 + 2° (at most 95°), or from the sensor diagonal when no FOV is given.

Hyperfocal distance
H = f²/(N c) + f, circle of confusion c = 5 µm (typical for 3 µm pixels); not given when the aperture N is not stated.
Unit calibration
Replaces the library lens for that camera everywhere (see Unit lens calibration).

Coverage and pixel density

What it computes. For every cell of a ground grid around the vehicle (the region of interest, ROI), which cameras see it and at what pixel density, and from those the coverage, blind area, redundancy and the stitching seams. Module: core/analysis.py.

seen(P) ⇔ (u, v) on the sensor ∧ θ ≤ θmax ∧ Dcube(P − C) > |P − C| − (0.02 + 0.01·|P − C|) C the lens's position, Dcube the occlusion cube map's depth in that direction (Occlusion); the allowance of 2 cm + 1 % of the distance keeps a point on a surface from hiding itself.
ρpx/cm(θ, R) = √( r′(θ) · r(θ)/sin θ · a ) / p / (100 R) The pixel density on a target facing the camera at range R (m): the geometric mean of the radial (dr/dθ) and tangential (r/sin θ) sampling in pixels per radian, divided by the range. Its square is the number of pixels a square centimetre facing the camera covers. For a pinhole it is f/(p cos1.5θ)/(100 R).
Grid
Cells of 0.05 m (setting) within an offset (default 2 m) of the body's outline or bounding box, or a polygon; cells under the body left out. A cell is evaluated at its centre, at the ground or at an obstacle height.
Figures
Coverage % (cells seen by any camera), blind area (m²), multi-coverage %, the lowest density over the covered cells, the shares above the minimum (default 3 px/cm) and target (default 5 px/cm) densities, and per camera the area only it sees and what removing it would cost.
Seams
Between cameras adjacent in azimuth about the body centre: the direction that keeps the most cells seen by both with the higher of the two densities, unless set by hand; a linear blend of blend_m across it.

Occlusion by the body

What it computes. For every direction from a camera's (or sensor's) position, how far away the vehicle body, the rig and the other sensors' housings are: a cube map of depths. Every study that asks "is this point hidden" looks it up. Module: core/occlusion.py, housings core/housings.py.

The body (simplified to about 8000 triangles) and the rig parts are rasterised onto the six faces of a cube around the position, 256 × 256 texels per face (about 0.35° at a face's centre), far triangles first by the distance of each triangle's nearest point (the painter's algorithm), each in a colour that encodes its index. A texel then holds the exact distance along its centre ray to the plane of the triangle drawn there (metres, 32-bit float), never more than that triangle's farthest corner. Sensor and camera housings are composited in exactly: a ray test of the housing's solid (its library box or cylinder) at every texel inside its footprint, the body kept in front where a triangle lies nearer or less than 3 cm behind the housing's face. A map depends on the position only, so turning a camera reuses it (environment mapping, Greene 1986).

texel of a direction d: face = the axis of max |di| and its sign;  (s, t) = (d·right, d·up)/(d·forward) ∈ [−1, 1]²;  depth t = n·(v0 − p) / (n·dc) n the drawn triangle's unit normal, v0 a corner, p the position, dc the texel's centre ray

Perception range

What it computes. How far out, in each direction around the vehicle, the rig resolves the objects a driving dataset needs, judged by their height in pixels. Module: core/perception.py.

hpx = ‖π(G + ztop) − π(G + zbottom)‖,   wpx = ‖π(G + ½wn) − π(G − ½wn)‖ π the camera's full projection (lens model, pose); G the object's ground point, n square to the line of sight; exact through the lens, no small-angle approximation. For a pinhole, hpx = f·H/Z with Z the depth along the axis.

An object is resolved by a camera when both ends are in the image and the lens circle, its centre is not hidden (the cube map) and hpx ≥ minpx. Along each of 360 azimuths about the vehicle's centre, ranges from 1 m to 250 m are sampled (log-spaced) and the far range is the last resolved one, refined between it and the next sample by interpolating 1/hpx linearly in range (exact for a pinhole). The rig's range is the best camera's; eight 45° sectors are judged against required ranges.

Critical targets and mounting envelopes

What it computes. Whether the rig resolves objects at places it must (a child 2 m ahead of the bumper, a kerb by the front wheel, a traffic light at a junction), and how far each camera's mount may be off before it stops resolving them. Module: core/critical.py.

A standing target is a vertical rectangle square to the line of sight; a kerb a vertical face along a line; a lane marking a painted strip, straight or curved. Each is sampled as stations (columns across a target, points along a kerb) with nodes along the span its requirement measures. A node is seen when it projects inside the image and the lens circle and the straight line to it meets no body, rig or housing triangle (tested exactly, ray against triangle and solid, not through the cube maps, since the envelopes move the camera). A station's pixels are the summed projected length of its seen segments; a target is met when at least min_share of it is seen, its pixels reach min_px and its distance lies in the lens's focus range.

Envelopes. For every camera and each target it meets, one pose axis at a time is moved both ways (yaw, pitch, roll in 0.25° steps; x, y, z in steps growing from 0.5 mm) until the target is lost, and that step bisected to 0.01° or 0.1 mm. The joint check tries all six axes at once on the 3⁶ = 729-pose grid of (−, 0, +) offsets.

Lidar beams and points

What it computes. Where each lidar channel meets the ground, what of the beams lands on the own vehicle, the ground point density, and how many points per frame fall on an object at a given place, hence each lidar's range on each object class. Modules: core/sensors.py, core/sensor_coverage.py.

d(ε, α) = Rs · (cos ε cos α, cos ε sin α, sin ε),   ground ring of a level lidar: r = h / tan|ε| ε a channel's elevation (the datasheet's, or the measured beam table when loaded), α the azimuth counter-clockwise from the sensor's +x, Rs the sensor's rotation (REP 103 axes), h its height.
points on an object ≈ Σchannels crossing it (angular width / Δα),   angular width = 2 atan(w/2d) Δα the azimuth step (h_res) at the frame rate. A channel crosses the object when its elevation in the lidar's own frame at that azimuth lies between the object's bottom and top, its beam is not blocked by the vehicle before it, and the distance is within the range on a 10 % target and beyond the minimum range.

Beams are traced against the same cube map as the cameras from the lidar's optical centre; a hit within 5 cm is its own mount and ignored. Each traced beam (every 0.25° of azimuth) stands for 0.25°/Δα real points, which gives the ground density map (points per m² per frame). The lidar range of a class is the farthest range at which the points summed over all lidars reach the class's minimum (typical: 20 points on a car, 8 on a pedestrian, 10 on a cyclist, 5 on a sign).

Range figures
The datasheet range on a dark (10 %) target; brighter targets reach further as √(ρ/0.10) (the lidar equation for an extended target), capped at the rated maximum (sample dataset).
Library
Nominal datasheet figures of named products (Ouster, Hesai, Velodyne, Livox), each with its source in its note.

Radar range and the bumper fascia

What it computes. Whether a radar detects an object at a place, from its zones (a range on a car and the half-angles of its field), the object's radar cross-section and what the radar looks through. Modules: core/sensors.py, core/sensor_coverage.py, core/materials.py.

detected ⇔ |α| ≤ αzone ∧ |ε| ≤ εzone ∧ R ≤ Rzone · (σ / 10 m²)¼ · (T²)¼ The radar equation: received power ∝ σ/R⁴, so at a fixed detection threshold the range scales with σ¼ and with the fourth root of the two-way power transmission T² of the fascia and its paint (Skolnik). Nothing of the vehicle may lie between, beyond 0.15 m from the radar (the fascia it sits behind, for a radar the materials study finds inside the body behind a cover; for one outside the body or behind metal every surface counts).

The fascia. A planar multilayer: the bumper's substrate and the paint (primer, basecoat, clear coat), each layer a complex permittivity ε′(1 − j tan δ) and a thickness, solved with the characteristic-matrix method for TE and TM at the ray's incidence (Macleod, ch. 2; Born & Wolf §1.6), the two polarisations' powers averaged.

Mj = [[cos δj, i sin δj/ηj], [i ηj sin δj, cos δj]],  δj = k0 Nj dj cos θj,  ηj = Nj cos θj (TE), Nj/cos θj (TM) [B, C]T = ΠMj · [1, η0]T; r = (η0B − C)/(η0B + C), t = 2η0/(η0B + C); T = |t|². Each zone is derated by the mean two-way transmission over its unblocked rays. The cavity between the radar's face (taken as metal, the worst case) and the fascia builds up as 1/|1 + r e−j2kg cos θ|²: the multipath risk.
Cross-sections (typical)
Car 10 m² (the datasheet ranges are on a car), pedestrian 0.5 m², cyclist 1 m²; traffic signs left to the other sensors (σ = 0).
Radars
Typical classes, not products: long range 250 m within ±9° (±7° in elevation) with a 70 m, ±45° near zone; corner 100 m within ±75° (±10°).
Materials
Permittivities and loss tangents of the bumper compounds (PP/EPDM, PC, PC/ABS) and paint layers at 76–81 GHz are typical values from published measurements (Emilsson 2017 for the paints; each class names its source in the app); glass's conductivity follows ITU-R P.2040 (σ = 0.0036 f1.3394 S/m, f in GHz).
Corner reflectors
A triangular trihedral of edge a: σ = 4πa⁴/(3λ²) (Knott et al.).

e-con lens datasheets

e-con Systems' STURDeCAM31 lens datasheet (Rev 1.2, August 2024) and module datasheet (Rev 1.4, June 2024), the documents listed for download on the camera's product page, carry no confidentiality marking. They state the two lenses' focal lengths (H05R2 2.62 mm, H03R2 5.5 mm), apertures (F2.0), fields of view (the figures the library already uses) and focus ranges (0.35 m and 1.28 m to infinity), but no measured image height against field angle. The built-in H05R2 and H03R2 therefore stay fitted to the stated field of view (see Lens models); a measured distortion table from e-con, or a unit calibration, would replace the fit.
